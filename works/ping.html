Ping, Mr. & Mrs. Crow, and Other Neighbours

Multi channel video installation with sound, dur:12 min, 2026



It would seem then that the idea that humans are the only story-telling animals is by no means an unproblematic reflection of reality. It is something that some people like to believe, just as some once believed that most humans were brutes and thus incapable of making meaning. It is, in other words, a construct , one that is intimately connected with structures of power and with the forceful repression of the awareness of nonhuman forms of agency and expression. Not surprisingly, in this matter, too, the hand of power has often fallen hardest on Indigenous people.







It is perfectly possible, then, that far from being an exclusively human attribute, the narrative faculty is the most animal of human abilities, a product of one of the traits that humans indisputably share with animals and many other beings – attachements to place. Perhaps, then, story telling, far from setting humans apart from animals, is actually the most important residue of our formerly wild selves.

Amitav Ghosh THE NUTMEG'S CURSE



That animals are sentient is no longer in doubt, with one clarification: the physical, three-dimensional space we share is the same as theirs, but our inner worlds are different. They are equally complex, yet we cannot experience exactly the same feelings as a bird that sees the world from above and has no concept of what a wall or border means, or an octopus, which lives underwater, has a nervous system distributed throughout its body, and thinks with its tentacles. To suggest otherwise would be to fall into the kind of anthropomorphism found in Walt Disney films.



The work does not tell us something we do not already know. Rather, it highlights what we tend to overlook: situations that happen every day around us but to which we pay little attention. This is, after all, one of the functions of art. The crows’ relentless pursuit of Ping, my ginger cat, challenged my preconceptions. There seemed to be no logic to it, if we take for granted that every animal’s primary goal is survival. On the contrary, this constant preoccupation with him potentially exposed the crows to danger. It was clear that their pursuit was driven by inner needs and emotions. I could use words such as revenge, rage, or justice to describe their behaviour, but that, too, would amount to a form of anthropomorphism.



Was the poisoned bee aware of the injustice? Most likely not. Yet it might well have perceived that its death was unnatural. Some years ago, I made a short video for YouTube about the devastating consequences of glyphosate, in which I included, among other things, footage of the spraying of wild flowers and bees dying of toxicity. I titled it Backstreet Genocides. Today, with genocide once again a reality, that title sounds tactless, to say the least. And yet, it brings to mind the idea that, in order to treat our fellow human beings properly, we might first need to treat the rest of nature properly. Or perhaps it is the other way around.




The work would not have been the same without the music composition of Galina Dimova-Georgieva and the help, in orchestrating it, of Nicola Georgiev.
